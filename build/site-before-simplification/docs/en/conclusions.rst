Concluding remarks
==================

This paper presented a pedagogical path for the probabilistic modeling of real physical data with the generalized lambda distribution, in the FKML parameterization, fitted by the method of moments and compared with classical distributions pertinent to each quantity.

The two examples led to different conclusions, and this difference is their pedagogical value. For the daily mean wind speed, the flexibility of the GLD made a difference. The Rayleigh distribution, with a fixed coefficient of variation, does not describe the data. The Weibull improves the fit considerably, and the GLD had the smallest deviations at the three quantiles examined, smaller than the recording step and of the same order as the resampling variability. The simulations show that rounding changes the scale of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance considerably, but they do not allow the observed discrepancy to be apportioned among its possible causes. The same example, however, showed that reproducing the four sample moments does not guarantee a support compatible with the observations, since the fitted model excludes the days of weakest wind (0.95% of the sample), which makes its likelihood zero. For the July temperature, the GLD also comes closer to the data, but the discrepancies of the normal are small relative to the sampling variability estimated for the quantiles, and the two-parameter model remains a parsimonious alternative for this description. The conclusions hold for the variables, the time windows, and the estimation method analyzed.

The quantile formulation proved advantageous in three respects. Percentiles are obtained directly and read in the units of the quantity, which makes the comparison between models interpretable in m/s or in :math:`^\circ`\ C. Sampling by inverse transform is immediate, which makes it directly usable in uncertainty propagation by Monte Carlo simulation :ref:`[26] <ref-gums1>`, including when the input quantity is skewed or bounded. And the parameters have visualizable effects on the shape of the distribution. On the other hand, the density and the cumulative distribution function require numerical procedures, and the fit by moments requires checking the validity, the moment domain, the support, and the goodness of fit of each solution.

The material addresses recurring conceptual difficulties in teaching, such as distinguishing density from probability, interpreting quantiles, understanding high-order moments, recognizing that numerical convergence does not guarantee goodness of fit, separating statistical adequacy from physical explanation, comparing discrepancies with the measurement resolution and with the sampling uncertainty, and perceiving the effects of the time window and of temporal aggregation. Because it has not been applied in a classroom, the material does not allow claims of learning gains. The sequence in Section :ref:`6 <sec:roteiro>` is a proposal, and its evaluation with students is left as a next step.

Code :ref:`1 <lst:quantil>` to Code :ref:`3 <lst:avaliacao>` show the essentials of the procedure. The complete pedagogical notebook, which reproduces all the figures, tables, and simulations of this paper with explanations between the steps, together with the code excerpts and the data, accompanies the paper as supplementary material, with execution instructions.
