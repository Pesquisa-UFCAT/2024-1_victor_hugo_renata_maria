### A B S T R A C T

Climate change accelerates carbonation in concrete, raising risks of cracking and spalling. However, existing model formulations often oversimplify this impact, inadequately representing the intrinsically non-linear and phase-dependent behavior of the carbonation process. In this paper, we propose a novel framework that integrates Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and a Predictive Markov Decision Process with Phase-type Approximation (PMDP-PH), enabling resilient and cost-effective infrastructure maintenance under carbonation risks. PINNs embed governing physical laws within neural architectures, enabling accurate inference of carbonation dynamics even under limited observational data. This framework accommodates non-exponential sojourn time distributions in both the initiation and propagation phases, effectively approximated using hypo-exponential models. The PMDP-PH adaptively updates inspection and maintenance strategies by continuously refining the remaining useful life (RUL) distribution in real time. This decision-making process is formulated as a tractable multi-stage model predictive control (MPC) problem over selected belief states, ensuring a non-decreasing value function throughout the robust horizon. Applied to a representative infrastructure system, our method reduces total maintenance costs by up to 61.9% compared to benchmark strategies under variable deterioration scenarios. These findings highlight the promise of combining physics-informed learning with a new form of predictive control strategy to strengthen infrastructure resilience under the growing threat of carbonation-induced deterioration.

#### **1. Introduction**

Infrastructure components play a vital role in enhancing societal well-being, with the service life of these systems heavily dependent on the resilience of key materials, such as reinforced concrete [1]. As urbanization accelerates and the global population continues to grow, atmospheric concentrations of carbon dioxide are projected to rise significantly, leading to increased global temperatures. Climate change is expected to adversely affect the durability of concrete structures, potentially reducing the lifespan of existing infrastructure. For example, global warming could result in a 31% increase in structural damage to bridges, potentially shortening their service life by up to 15 years [2]. Consequently, the development of cost-effective maintenance strategies to mitigate the impacts of climate change is critical.

Carbonation is a major cause of deterioration in aging infrastructure, and its progression is further intensified by the effects of global warming. Existing studies commonly categorize the carbonation process into three distinct stages: initiation, propagation, and failure [3]. The initiation phase is marked by the ingress of carbon dioxide, which

causes the de-passivation of reinforcement materials [4]. This phase is primarily governed by diffusion, with its rate significantly heightened by global warming and increased carbon emissions. However, pinpointing the exact end of the initiation phase is challenging due to limited inspection technologies, which can affect the evaluation of state resilience during the propagation phase and influence subsequent maintenance strategies. When the carbon dioxide concentration at the surface of the steel surpasses a critical threshold, the propagation phase commences [5]. In this phase, the rate of deterioration of the steel increases, potentially leading to a range of failure mechanisms such as cracking and spalling.

Climate change is accelerating the carbonation of concrete structures, thereby heightening the likelihood of cracking, spalling, and eventual structural failure. Current predictive models struggle to represent the inherently nonlinear and phase-dependent mechanisms driving carbonation. To our knowledge, research on developing adaptive and intelligent inspection and maintenance strategies specifically targeting this deterioran process remains limited. To bridge this gap, we

## 2. Literature review

Carbonation corrosion is one of the common deterioration patterns of reinforced concrete. Contemporary research on deterioration mechanisms can generally be categorized into three major directions: physics-based, data-based, and hybrid approaches. In terms of the physics-based approach, the carbonation process is divided into three phases. The first stage is the diffusion of carbon dioxide, and the second stage is the corrosion of the steel until failure. Many scholars have conducted in-depth experimental studies on the factors that influence carbonation. Bao et al. conducted an experimental investigation together with multi-physics modeling of supercritical carbonation, aiming to examine how heterogeneously distributed coarse aggregates and material porosity influence the non-uniform penetration depth of carbonation in concrete [6]. Iwama and Maekawa developed a thermo-chemo-physical model across multiple scales to describe carbonation, decarbonation, and re-carbonation phenomena occurring during and following exposure to elevated temperatures [7]. The results from the physics-based approach are restricted to preset experimental conditions. In actual deterioration processes, environmental conditions fluctuate. The results based on physics models cannot be adapted to actual deterioration conditions. In terms of the data-based approaches, Duan and Cao introduced machine-learning-based models for carbonation and subsequently simplified them into a practical framework that demonstrated strong predictive accuracy on both accelerated and natural carbonation experiments [8]. Couto et al. employed Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) to estimate carbonation depth, utilizing a synthetic dataset of 20,000 samples derived from the validated Possan formulation [9]. Machine learning and deep learning can help predict the carbonation deterioration. However, in practice, high-quality data is hardly obtained, especially for the carbonation process. In addition, the lack of interpretability also limits the application of data-driven approaches.

As for the hybrid approach, Li et al. developed a hybrid surrogate approach, termed DL-LGBM-DRS, that merges deep learning with

PINNs provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating the sojourn time distribution. Climate change is accelerating carbonation in concrete infrastructure, increasing the risks of cracking, spalling, and structural failure, which requires effective maintenance strategies to mitigate the effect and avoid failure. In the extant literature, carbonation is often modeled as a Markov process [13] for optimizing maintenance. However, the real sojourn time of the carbonation condition is non-exponentially distributed in dynamic environments. Hence, the semi-Markov process [14] is proposed to model general distributed deterioration. Zambon et al. integrated a semi-Markov process with the carbonation mechanism to forecast deterioration [15]. Wang and Miao developed an optimal strategy for preventive maintenance by constructing a model based on the semi-Markov framework, specifically tailored for a balanced system [16]. Zheng et al. enhanced condition-based maintenance strategies by employing dynamic threshold adjustments within a system framework modeled using the proportional hazards approach, structured under a Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) framework [17].

Visual inspection is commonly employed to detect deterioration caused by carbonation; however, it is often hindered by significant measurement uncertainty [18]. Consequently, the accuracy of the semi-Markov model in modeling deterioration is influenced. A solving approach is to incorporate inspection uncertainty into the model formulation, such as the Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), Partially Observable Semi-Markov Decision Process (POSMDP), Hidden Markov Model (HMM), and Hidden Semi-Markov Model (HSMM). Zhang et al. presented an innovative framework for optimizing sequential inspection strategies in hierarchical multistate systems, where the decision-making process is modeled using a finite-horizon POMDP [19]. Cheng et al. represented a safety-critical system with partial observability using a hidden continuous-time Markov model, and proposed an integrated optimization approach that combines inspection scheduling with a condition-based mission abort strategy [20]. Guo and Liang introduced a predictive Markov decision process to enhance inspection and maintenance policies for multi-state systems with limited observability, achieved by integrating elements of both HMM and POMDP [21]. Arcieri et al. integrated Bayesian decision theory with POMDPs to develop a resilient maintenance planning approach that accounts for model uncertainty, specifically applied to railway system management [22]. Compare et al. designed a decision-support system grounded in POMDPs to guide operational planning in Gas Transmission Networks (GTNs). This framework incorporates degradation estimates generated by Prognostics and Health Management systems, while accounting for fluctuating demand and the impact of operational choices on the progression of system deterioration [23]. Khaleghei and Kim applied phase-type approximation to convert POSMDP into POMDP and identified structural properties to aid in solving [24]. Chen et al. designed an HMM incorporating autocorrelated observational data to effectively model the degradation processes in manufacturing systems [25]. Liu and Zhu introduced an innovative switching HSMM to depict the progression of equipment deterioration across different operational modes over time [26]. Eleftheroglou et al. introduced an advanced Similarity Learning HSMM for prognostics, designed to improve prediction accuracy, particularly when encountering anomalous patterns not represented in the training dataset [27].

The POMDP, POSMDP, HMM, and HSMM are often implemented under the scheme of periodic inspection. However, carbonation follows stochastic patterns. In the early stages, systems often operate normally and may not require immediate maintenance. Conducting inspections during this phase may not contribute to improving conditions. Conversely, as deterioration advances, it can escalate rapidly, potentially leading to unexpected failures between scheduled inspections. Hence, to address the risk of carbonation affected by climate change, the existing inspection and maintenance strategy requires to be readjusted. The development of intelligent and adaptive inspection and maintenance strategies offers significant potential for the future. Duan et al. proposed a maintenance approach that adapts to changing environmental conditions, utilizing revised conditional reliability estimates for mechanical systems [28]. Yang et al. introduced a comprehensive global-dynamic maintenance strategy tailored for systems composed of multiple deteriorating components. This approach enables immediate updating of the system's overall health status following the execution of each group maintenance operation [29].

In this paper, we aim to design a hybrid approach to accurately predict the deterioration trend and RUL, and to formulate intelligent and adaptive maintenance strategies for carbonation even in the face of climate change.

## 3. Model formulation for carbonation

To recalibrate the inspection and maintenance strategy for carbonation, we model the carbonation process as a surrogate partially observable Markov process. The carbonation process is divided into initiation and propagation phases. The evaluation of their sojourn time distributions is first performed using a PINN alongside a competing failure model. The resulting non-exponential sojourn time distributions are subsequently approximated by a phase-type distribution. This transformation enables the carbonation process to be represented as a surrogate partially observable Markov process, facilitating the incorporation of both stochastic deterioration and partial observability in the model. The formulation framework is demonstrated in Fig. 1.

### 3.1. Evaluation of sojourn time distributions for carbonation

The initiation phase is characterized by the infiltration of carbon dioxide. When the concentration on the steel surface reaches a critical value, this marks the onset of the propagation phase. The governing partial differential equation for a one-dimensional transport process involving carbon dioxide in the initiation phase can be expressed as [30]

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D_s \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} - v\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} - K_T C \tag{1}$$

where $C$ represents the concentration of carbon dioxide (in g/m$^3$). $x$ refers to diffusion depth (in m), and $t$ is the time (in s). $\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}$ represents the change rate of the concentration of carbon dioxide over time. Eq. (1) incorporates the coupling of multiple mechanisms, including diffusion, convection, and reaction. $D_s$ is the diffusion coefficient (in m$^2$/s). $\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}$ denotes spatial diffusion of the concentration of carbon dioxide. $v$ describes convective transport, where carbon dioxide is carried by the bulk motion of pore water (in m/s). $\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$ expresses the concentration of carbon dioxide gradient along depth. $K_T$ expresses the reaction rate constant for first-order decay at a specified temperature (in 1/s). The initial and boundary conditions are specified as $C(x,0) = C_k$, $C(0,t) = C_s$, and $C(L,t) = C_f$, where $C_k$, $C_s$ and $C_f$ correspond to the initial carbon dioxide concentration within the concrete, at the surface of the concrete structure, and at the interface between the concrete and steel, respectively. $L$ represents the thickness of the concrete cover (in m).

### 3.2. Phase-type approximation

The hypo-exponential distribution describes the random time it takes for a Markov process to reach its absorbing state. Consider a continuous-time Markov process $\{X(t); t \geq 0\}$ with $n+1$ states, where the first $n$ states are transient and state $n+1$ is absorbing. The initial state of the process is described by the probability vector $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, a_0)$, where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ is a $1 \times n$ vector and $a_0$ is a scalar. The hypo-exponential distribution describes the time interval between the start of the process and its absorption into the absorbing state. The probability of transitioning between states within the time interval $\Delta t$ is expressed as Eq. (5).

$$P\{X(t+\Delta t) = i+1 \mid X(t) = i\} = \lambda_i \Delta t + o(\Delta t) \tag{5}$$

where $\lambda_i$ represents the transition rates from state $i = 1, 2, \ldots, n$ to the next state. Hence, the transition rate matrix $Q$ follows the structure as

Q = \begin{bmatrix} -\lambda_1 & \lambda_1 & 0 & \vdots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\lambda_2 & \lambda_2 & \ldots & 0 & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \vdots & -\lambda_{n-1} & \lambda_{n-1} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \vdots & 0 & -\lambda_n \end{bmatrix} \tag{6}
$$

The coefficient of variation (CV) of a hypo-exponential distribution is expressed as Eq. (7) [31,32].

$$
CV = \frac{Var^2(t)}{E^2(t)} = \frac{E(t^2) - E^2(t)}{E^2(t)} \tag{7}
$$

where $E(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\lambda_i}$, $E(t^2) = 2\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\lambda_i} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\lambda_k}$. Hence, $CV < 1$.

**Proposition 1 (hypo-exponential approximation).** *The RUL distribution, approximated using a hypo-exponential model characterized by a probability vector **α** and a transition rate matrix $Q$, can be represented as follows:*

$$
F(t) = 1 - \boldsymbol{\alpha} e^{Qt} \mathbf{1} \tag{8}
$$

Proposition 1 (hypo-exponential approximation) helps predict the RUL for a continuous-time Markov process. The entire corrosion process driven by carbonation can be divided into three phases: initiation, propagation, and eventual failure. As shown in Proposition 2 (hypo-exponential approximation), the hypo-exponential distribution preserves the closure property. Therefore, when this distribution is utilized for approximation, the overall deterioration process continues to conform to the principles of Markov modeling.

**Proposition 2 (hypo-exponential approximation).** *Let $PH_1$ be a hypo-exponential distribution defined by the initial probability vector $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_1$, the transition rate matrix $Q_1$, and the failure rate vector $\boldsymbol{q}_1 = -Q_1\mathbf{1}$ and $PH_2$ be an independent hypo-exponential distribution characterized by $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_2$, $Q_2$, and $\boldsymbol{q}_2$. The convolution $PH_1 + PH_2$ then results in another hypo-exponential distribution with the following properties.*

$$\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\alpha}_1 & \vdots & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{9}$$

$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} Q_1 & \vdots & \boldsymbol{q}_1\boldsymbol{\alpha}_2 \\ \cdots & \cdots & \cdots \\ 0 & \vdots & Q_2 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{10}$$

As indicated in Proposition 2 (hypo-exponential approximation), the sojourn time distribution, which is non-exponential in nature, can be effectively approximated using a hypo-exponential distribution. This allows the semi-Markov deterioration process to be reformulated as an equivalent Markov model, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

The original carbonation contains three phases: initiation phase ($P_1$), propagation phase ($P_2$), and failure ($F$). We utilize $S_{11}, S_{12}, \ldots, S_{1n_1}$ and $S_{21}, S_{22}, \ldots, S_{2n_2}$ to represent the surrogate states of $P_1$ and $P_2$ after hypo-exponential approximation. The state notation is defined as $S_{ki}$, where $k$ expresses the phase and $i$ represents the surrogate state. Failure indicates the end of carbonation and requires no hypo-exponential approximation. For uniform notations, the failure is expressed as $S_{31}$ in the surrogate partially observable Markov process. The whole state set is defined as $S$. The observations of $P_1$ and $P_2$ are denoted as $O_1$ and $O_2$. The failure is self-revealing in our formulation. To match the surrogate Markov process, we need to discretize $O_1$ and $O_2$ into $n_1$ and $n_2$ observations based on the proportion of the mean sojourn time of the surrogate Markov states. For uniform denotation, the observation of failure $F$ is defined as $O_{31}$. Hence, the discretized observations of the partially observable Markov process are denoted as $\mathcal{O} = \{O_{11}, \ldots, O_{1n_1}, O_{21}, \ldots, O_{2n_2}, O_{31}\}$. To apply the hypo-exponential approximation, the parameters ($\lambda$ and $n$) are required to be assessed by moment matching. The $m$th moment of hypo-exponential distribution is expressed as

$$E(t^m) = (-1^m)\, m!\, \boldsymbol{\alpha} Q^{-m} \mathbf{1} \tag{11}$$

The parameters $\lambda$ and $n$ of the hypo-exponential distribution are determined by aligning its mean, standard deviation, and skewness with those of the target distribution. Hence, we set the goal of the objective function, as presented in Eq. (12), to reduce the deviation value in the mean, standard deviation, and skewness between the target distribution and its hypo-exponential approximation.

$$\arg\min_{\lambda_i, n} w_n = (\hat{\mu}_n - \mu)^2 + (\hat{\sigma}_n - \sigma)^2 + (\hat{SK}_n - SK)^2 \tag{12}$$

where $\hat{\mu}_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i^{-1}$, $\hat{\sigma}_n = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i^{-2}}$ and $\hat{SK}_n = \dfrac{2\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i^{-3}}{\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i^{-2}\right\}^{3/2}}$.

The hypo-exponential approximation will introduce an error in the model formulation. Here, we quantify the error as the deviation value $w_n$. ## 4. Optimization of inspection and maintenance strategy

We propose a PMDP-PH approach to optimize inspection and maintenance strategies for mitigating carbonation effects. The proposed method updates inspection and maintenance strategies in real time, leveraging RUL estimations derived from partial observations. To further enhance the efficiency of the approach, we incorporate robust decision epochs to reduce computational complexity, thereby facilitating scalable and effective decision-making for strategies.

To cope with carbonation under climate change conditions, we reschedule the timings of inspections, represented as $\mathcal{T}_p = \{t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_p\}$. Due to the limitations of current inspection technologies, the deterioration state can only be partially observed. The relationship between actual deterioration and observable states is represented by the emission probability mapping, denoted as $E\left(O_{t_d}|S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right)$. The state–action transition probability is expressed as $P\left(S_{t_{d+1}} = S_{k'i'}|S_{t_d} = S_{ki}, a_l\right)$. In contrast to the perfect observation scenario in a Markov Decision Process (MDP), the PMDP-PH utilizes belief states to capture our estimates of the deterioration, which are derived from inspection data. The belief is defined as $\mathbf{B}\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right) = P\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}|O_0, O_{t_1}, \ldots, O_{t_d}\right)$.

where $c_I$ denotes the cost of the inspection ($a_I$). The PMDP-PH is an online maintenance update model. Hence, we need to consider all possible observations after the time $t_d$ with periodic decision interval

where $q_{S_{ki}}^{a_l} = c_I + c_l$. The initial condition is set as

where $q_{S_{ki}}(T)$ signifies the terminal value of the systems. Given that belief states are continuous, calculating the value function for these states can be computationally intensive. To simplify this process, we introduce Proposition 2, which addresses the value function.

**Proposition 2.** The optimized value of PMDP-PH is piecewise linear and convex expressed as

$$V\left(\mathbf{B}(t_d)\right) = \min_{\mathcal{L}} \left[\sum_{S_{ki}} \boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right) \mathbf{B}\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right)\right], t_d < T \tag{19}$$

where $\mathcal{L}$ is a function about $O_{t_d+\Delta}$ and $a_l$. $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right)$ is iteratively calculated as Eq. (20).

$$\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right) = q_{S_{ki}}^{a_l} + \gamma \sum_{S_{k'i'}, O_{t_d+\Delta}} P\left(S_{t_d+\Delta} = S_{k'i'}|S_{t_d}, a_l\right) E\left(O_{t_d+\Delta}|S_{t_d+\Delta}\right) \boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}\left(S_{t_d+\Delta}\right), t_d < T \tag{20}$$

The initial condition is set as $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}\left(S_T = S_{ki}\right) = q_{S_{ki}}(T)$.

The proof of Proposition 2 is provided in Appendix B. Proposition 2 shows that the process of iterating the value function can be reformulated as the determination of the $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$-vectors for the corresponding belief states. These $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$-vectors encode the optimal maintenance strategies. In the context of a continuous belief state space, the $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$-vectors partition the space into distinct regions, with each region associated with a specific optimal maintenance action. This approach simplifies the computation of the value function.

**Proposition 3.** The optimal $V\left(\mathbf{B}(t_d)\right)$ of the PMDP-PH is non-decreasing with respect to the stochastic increasing of $\mathbf{B}(t_d)$.

The proof of Proposition 3 is found in Appendix C. The definition of stochastic increasing is as follows: For vector $\mathbf{X} = [x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n]$ and $\mathbf{Y} = [y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n]$, $\mathbf{X}$ is said to be stochastically smaller than $\mathbf{Y}$, denoted by $\mathbf{X} \le_{SI} \mathbf{Y}$ if the condition $\sum_{i=k}^{n} x_i \le \sum_{i=k}^{n} y_i$ for any $0 \le k \le n$ is satisfied. Proposition 3 indicates that the optimal strategy in the PMDP-PH is a form of control-limit strategy.

Traditional value iteration and policy iteration algorithms are not practical for partially observable Markov deteriorating processes because of the significant computational complexity arising from the continuous belief state space. To address this, we propose a Predictive Point-Based Value Iteration (PPBVI) algorithm, which extends the Point-Based Value Iteration (PBVI) method by incorporating adaptive inspections. We focus on specific belief states along the deterioration trajectory. The representativeness of the selected belief states is measured by the gap, defined as $gap = \max\left(V\left(\tilde{B}\right) - V(B)\right)$. $\tilde{B}$ represents the chosen belief states, and $B$ denotes the full continuous belief states. As demonstrated by Proposition 2, the continuous belief state space can be partitioned into multiple regions. The value function can then be reformulated as Eq. (21).

$$V\left(\mathbf{B}(t_d)\right) = \min_{a_l \in \mathcal{A}} \left[\sum_{S_{ki}} \mathbf{B}\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right) q_{S_{ki}}^{a_l} + \min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}} \gamma \sum_{S_{ki}} \mathbf{B}\left(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}\right) \sum_{S_{k'i'}, O_{t_d+\Delta}} P\left(S_{t_d+\Delta} = S_{k'i'}|S_{t_d}, a_l\right) E\left(O_{t_d+\Delta}|S_{t_d+\Delta}\right) \boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}\left(S_{t_d+\Delta}\right)\right] \tag{21}$$

By leveraging specific belief states together with $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}$-vector data, the optimal strategy can be determined. Next, we examine the computational complexity involved. At each decision point, each $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}$ vector produces $|\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{O}|$ new vectors for the next decision epoch, corresponding to all possible $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}$. In the algorithm, unnecessary $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}$ vectors can be pruned to mitigate computational demands. Importantly, the computation of $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}$ is independent of $B$, allowing the results to be cached and reused for different belief states in $B$. The computation of each $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}$ necessitates $O(|S|)$ operations to compute inner products, as described in Eq. (19). The overall complexity for calculating the value function, as indicated by Eq. (21), is $O(|\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{O}| \times |B| \times |S|^2)$. When computing the optimal strategy for all $B$ using $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathcal{L}}$, the total number of operations required is $O(|\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{O}| \times |B|^2 \times |S|^2)$. The whole computation complexity in the finite horizon is $O(|\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{O}| \times |B|^2 \times |S|^2 \times |T|)$. In other words, as the number of decision epochs increases, the computational complexity grows rapidly. To mitigate the computation, we implement robust decision epochs to optimize inspection and maintenance online. The optimization of the PMDP-PH can be regarded as a special case of multi-stage MPC [33] in Proposition 4.

**Proposition 4.** Given $S$, $\mathcal{A}$, and $\mathcal{O}$, the optimization of the PMDP-PH with chosen belief states can be translated as a problem of multi-stage MPC.

The proof of Proposition 4 is found in Appendix D. Proposition 4 provides a link between the PMDP-PH model and the multi-stage MPC. The PMDP-PH model can be regarded as a special multi-stage MPC with contraction mapping and a non-decreasing value function. We assume that the uncertainty arising from deterioration, observations, and actions remains unchanged after a certain number of decision epochs, referred to as robust decision epochs, and persists until the end of the finite horizon. Hence, the computation complexity is reduced to $O(|\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{O}| \times |B|^2 \times |S|^2 \times |D_r|)$, which is smaller than $O(|\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{O}| \times |B|^2 \times |S|^2 \times |T|)$. Because robust decision epochs are smaller than the finite horizon $D_r < T$. The pseudocode for the PPBVI algorithm is provided in Algorithm 1.

In the PPBVI algorithm, a set of representative belief states is chosen to compute the value function. These belief states are derived from the uncertainty present in the multi-stage MPC models, based on discrete scenarios. Our approach is an iterative optimization process. The optimization is divided into two steps. The first step is to optimize the maintenance at the current inspection timing. In the second step, the next inspection timing is determined based on the reliability threshold and optimized action. Repeating the above two steps, we obtain the optimal inspection timings and maintenance strategy in the finite horizon.

# 5.3. Discussion

In recent years, research efforts have increasingly examined how to optimize inspection and maintenance planning using POMDP models under periodic inspection settings. The resulting decision framework is generally categorized as condition-based maintenance. Unlike the POMDP formulation, the PMDP-PH requires inspection intervals to be forecasted and optimized through RUL estimation, thereby positioning it as a predictive maintenance strategy. Our proposed method adaptively determines future inspection moments in real time according to the estimated RUL. Relative to conventional periodic inspection schemes, this method is capable of reducing redundant inspections. Based on the above discussion, three essential remarks can be highlighted:

1. As demonstrated in Fig. 8 and Table 4, the PMDP-PH framework adaptively modifies inspection intervals by incorporating the latest inspection information into its RUL estimates. This dynamic strategy contributes to a reduction in superfluous inspections.
2. Table 5 highlights that the PMDP-PH has more computational efficiency than the POMDP when the robust horizon is applied.
3. Fig. 9 and Table 6 illustrate that the PMDP-PH enables optimizing the maintenance strategies and reducing essential inspections under different emission and cost settings.

The rationale for cost reduction arises from the PMDP-PH model's ability to adjust inspection intervals according to outcomes of prior assessments, thereby minimizing unnecessary inspections over the system's service life. By more effectively utilizing available inspection information, the PMDP-PH framework supports predictive maintenance within the setting of partially observable Markov deterioration processes. Nonetheless, applying this method in practice is constrained by two main factors. The first relates to data availability, especially information describing system deterioration. In this study, the integration of physical knowledge helps to mitigate this issue. The second limitation concerns the resources required to carry out inspection and maintenance activities. Future work will examine how limited resources influence the feasibility of real-world applications.

# 6. Conclusion

This paper introduces an innovative methodology that merges PINNs with a PMDP-PH model to support efficient and resilient maintenance planning for infrastructure susceptible to carbonation. By incorporating fundamental physical principles into neural architectures, PINNs enable reliable inference of carbonation behavior, even when observational data is partially informative. The proposed model accounts for general, non-exponential sojourn time distributions during both the initiation and propagation phases of deterioration, which are approximated using hypo-exponential representations. The PMDP-PH framework facilitates the continuous adjustment of maintenance and inspection policies by updating the RUL distribution in real time. This adaptive mechanism is formulated as a multi-stage MPC problem over chosen belief states. This special multi-stage MPC is characterized by contraction mapping and a non-decreasing value function. When applied to a typical infrastructure system, the proposed approach results in a 61.9% reduction in overall maintenance expenditures relative to benchmark strategies, even under variable deterioration scenarios. These findings demonstrate the value of integrating physics-informed learning with a new form of predictive control strategy to bolster infrastructure resilience against increasing carbonation threats.

When applying our designed approach to multi-component systems, it is necessary to consider their internal interdependencies. Moreover, as the number of system components increases, the states, observations, and actions will rise accordingly. In these settings, multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithms will be designed to improve the computational efficiency in future research. We utilize the PINN to capture the deterioration by embedding the physics knowledge into the formulation. Our approach is adaptive to the changes in parameters caused by the types of concrete and environmental conditions. In the future, we will conduct further in-depth research on the changes in concrete types and environmental conditions, mainly focusing on our approach's adaptability.

## Appendix C. The proof of Proposition 3

**Proof.** We are tasked with determining whether to implement action $a_1$ or $a_2$. When $a_2$ is selected, the value $V_{a_2}$ remains fixed. To evaluate the value of $a_1$, we consider two limit scenarios. Scenario 1: If the system operates perfectly, the value $V_{a_1}^1$ is calculated as Eq. (C.1).

$$c_I + \sum_{S_0} \mathbf{B}(S_0) \sum_{S_{k'i'}} P(S_\Delta = S_{k'i'}|S_0,a_1) \sum_{O_\Delta} E(O_\Delta|S_\Delta) V(\mathbf{B}(\Delta)) \tag{C.1}$$

Scenario 2: If the system fails, the value $V_{a_1}^2$ is computed as Eq. (C.2).

$$c_I + \sum_{S_{ki}} \mathbf{B}(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}) \sum_{S_{31}} P(S_{t_d+\Delta} = S_{31}|S_{t_d},a_1) \sum_{O_{31}} E(O_{31}|S_{31}) V(\mathbf{B}(t_d+\Delta)) \tag{C.2}$$

We apply induction to show that if $\mathbf{B}(t_{d_1})$ is stochastic increasing than $\mathbf{B}(t_{d_2})$ under action $a_1$, the corresponding value functions also exhibit monotonic behavior. We initialize with $V([0,\dots,1]) = c_4$, where $c_4$ is a large constant. By this assumption, it follows that: $V(\mathbf{B}(t_{d_1})) - V(\mathbf{B}(t_{d_2})) \geq 0$. Finally, when the interval $\Delta$ is non-equidistant, Proposition 3 still holds. $\Box$

## Appendix D. The proof of Proposition 4

**Proof.** The multi-stage MPC can be represented using a scenario tree. In this framework, the scenario tree assumes a discrete-time formulation for an uncertain system, which can be expressed as:

$$x_{k+1}^c = f\left(x_k^j, u_k^j, d_k^r\right) \tag{D.1}$$

where each state $x_{k+1}^c$ at stage $k+1$ depends on the parent state $x_k^j$, the corresponding control input $u_k^j$, and the realization $r$ of the uncertainty at stage $k$, denoted by $d_k^r$. The scenario tree is represented by $I$, The multi-stage MPC problem can then be formulated as

$$J = \min_{x_k^j, u_k^j, \forall (j,k)\in I} \sum_{j \in S} w^j \left( v\left(x_N^j\right) + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} l\left(x_k^j, u_k^j\right) \right) \tag{D.2}$$

subject to:

$$x_{k+1}^c = f\left(x_k^j, u_k^j, d_k^r\right), \quad \forall (j,k) \in I\backslash I_{leaf} \tag{D.3a}$$

$$x_k^j \in \mathcal{X}, \quad \forall (j,k) \in I \tag{D.3b}$$

$$u_k^j \in \mathcal{U}, \quad \forall (j,k) \in I\backslash I_{leaf} \tag{D.3c}$$

$$x_N^j \in \mathcal{X}_f, \quad \forall (j,N) \in I \tag{D.3d}$$

where $l\left(x_k^j, u_k^j\right)$ represents the stage cost while $v\left(x_N^j\right)$ denotes the terminal penalty term. The states and control inputs are constrained to lie within the compact sets $\mathcal{X}$, $\mathcal{U}$, with $\mathcal{X}_f$ representing the terminal set. Additionally, the variable $w^j$ denotes the accumulated weight associated with the node $x_k^j$.

Eq. (16) defines the set of all beliefs that can be reached in a single step. By considering all possible one-step action-observation sequences from these beliefs, we can determine the beliefs that are reachable from $\mathbf{B}(0)$ in two steps, and so on. This process generates a tree structure with the initial belief state $\mathbf{B}(0)$ as the root. The space of reachable beliefs corresponds to all the nodes within this tree. In line with the formulation of multi-stage MPC, the PMDP-PH can thus be reformulated as:

$$Eq.(D.2) \to Eq.(17) \tag{D.4}$$

subject to:

$$Eq.(D.3a) \to \sum_{S_{ki},S_{k'i'},O_{t_d+\Delta}} \mathbf{B}(S_{t_d} = S_{ki}) P(S_{t_d+\Delta} = S_{k'i'}|S_{t_d},a_l) E(O_{t_d+\Delta}|S_{t_d+\Delta}),$$

$$\forall S_{t_d}, S_{t_d+\Delta} \in S, O_{t_d+\Delta} \in \mathcal{O}, a_l \in \mathcal{A}, \mathbf{B}(t_d) \in \widetilde{B}, t_d \in [0,T] \tag{D.5a}$$

$$Eq.(D.3b) \to \mathbf{B}(t_d) \in \widetilde{B}, \quad \forall t_d \in [0,T] \tag{D.5b}$$

$$Eq.(D.3c) \to a_l \in \mathcal{A} \tag{D.5c}$$

$$Eq.(D.3d) \to \mathbf{B}(T) \in \widetilde{B} \tag{D.5d}$$

The state $x$, control $u$, and uncertainty $d$ can be reformed as chosen belief state $\mathbf{B}$, action $a$, and transition probability $P$ and emission $E$. Eq. (D.2) can be regarded as Eq. (17). $v\left(x_N^j\right)$ can be transformed as $\mathbf{B}(T)\mathbf{q}(T)$. $l\left(x_k^j, u_k^j\right)$ is equivalent to the cost of action. Hence, the optimization of the PMDP-PH with chosen belief states can be equivalent to solving the problem of multi-stage MPC. $\Box$
