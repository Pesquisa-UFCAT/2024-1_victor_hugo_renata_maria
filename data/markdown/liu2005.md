# Multiobjective Maintenance Planning Optimization for Deteriorating Bridges Considering Condition, Safety, and Life-Cycle Cost

Min Liu, A.M.ASCE,<sup>1</sup> and Dan M. Frangopol, F.ASCE<sup>2</sup>

**Abstract:** Many of the currently available bridge management system tools focus on minimizing life-cycle maintenance cost of deteriorating bridges while imposing constraints on structural performance. The computed single optimal maintenance planning solution, however, may not necessarily meet a bridge manager's specific requirements on lifetime bridge performance. In this paper the life-cycle maintenance planning of deteriorating bridges is formulated as a multiobjective optimization problem that treats the lifetime condition and safety levels as well as life-cycle maintenance cost as separate objective functions. A multiobjective genetic algorithm is used as the search engine to automatically locate a large pool of different maintenance scenarios that exhibits an optimized tradeoff among conflicting objectives. This tradeoff provides improved opportunity for bridge managers to actively select the final maintenance scenario that most desirably balances life-cycle maintenance cost, condition, and safety levels of deteriorating bridges.

**DOI:** 10.1061/(ASCE)0733-9445(2005)131:5(833)

**CE Database subject headings:** Bridge maintenance; Life cycle cost; Optimization; Deterioration.

## Introduction

The economical allocation of limited funding for maintaining both safety and serviceability of deteriorating highway bridge networks constructed in the United States as well as other parts of the world necessitates suitable bridge maintenance system (BMS) tools that can maximize cost effectiveness. Available practical BMS software packages include *BRIDGIT* (Hawk and Small 1998) and *Pontis* (Thompson et al. 1998) systems that determine the timing and types of maintenance interventions based on discrete condition states resulting from visual inspections.

Numerical procedures have recently appeared in the literature that search for optimal maintenance planning. Most of the existing numerical procedures aim at minimizing the expected cumulative maintenance cost over a specified time horizon while treating other important bridge performance criteria as relevant constraints in the optimization process. For example, Kong and Frangopol (2003) presented a methodology for reliability-based life-cycle cost optimization of deteriorating bridges using the feasible search direction combined with the branching and multidimensional interpolation methods. Despite the advantage of these automated maintenance planning procedures that can provide a rational solution based on life-cycle cost minimization, one of the arguments against such approaches is that the computed single optimal maintenance solution may not necessarily meet a bridge manager's specific requirements on bridge performances. As a remedy, the numerical algorithms could be rerun using another set of constraints that is compatible with specified performance demands of the bridge manager. Facing a single maintenance solution, a bridge manager still has no opportunity to make judgmental decisions upon the choice of final maintenance scenario from a group of alternative ones that lead to distinct bridge performances other than merely emphasizing the life-cycle cost optimization issue.

A more powerful approach is to consider all relevant bridge performance aspects as separate objective functions, resulting in a multiobjective optimization formulation for the bridge maintenance planning problem. Instead of a unique optimal maintenance solution as is obtained from the traditional life-cycle cost optimization approaches, a set of alternative maintenance solutions is searched that comprises the best possible tradeoff among all competing objectives under consideration. Bridge managers' valuable experiences as well as personal preference can then be incorporated into the decision-making process to determine the compromise maintenance scenario that can most desirably balance different objectives. (2000) who investigated optimal maintenance of existing bridges by minimizing maintenance cost and maximizing bridge load-carrying capacity as well as durability; and Furuta et al. In this paper, life-cycle maintenance planning optimization of deteriorating bridges is formulated as a multiobjective problem to be solved by an evolutionary algorithm. Application examples using real data of reinforced concrete highway crossheads are provided to demonstrate the usefulness of the proposed procedure.

## Multiobjective Optimization Framework

The present study aims to develop an automated procedure that produces optimal bridge maintenance solutions while accounting for multiple different performance measures. Three objectives are considered in the present optimization problem: (1) improving the worst condition index value, (2) improving the worst safety index value over the intended service life, and (3) reducing the present value of the life-cycle maintenance cost. The evaluation methods are also described. A genetic algorithm (GA) is used as the numerical tool for solving the posed multiobjective optimization problem, which is conceptually described by the flowchart in Fig. 1.

### Condition and Safety Indices

The condition index used in this study is a continuous generalization (Neves and Frangopol 2005) of the visual inspection-based and discrete-valued quantities that are adopted in current bridge management systems. For example, Denton (personal communication 2002) assigns the following discrete condition values: 0=no chloride contamination, 1=onset of corrosion, 2=onset of cracking, and 3=loose concrete/significant delamination. Therefore, the larger value the condition index takes, the worse a bridge's condition is. The safety index in accordance with *DB12/01* (Highways Agency Standard for Bridge Assessment 2001) is quantified as the ratio of available to required live load-carrying capacity. The smaller value the safety index takes, the lower a bridge's safety level becomes.

A multilinear computational model (Frangopol et al. 2001) is used to depict deterioration profiles of structure performance in terms of condition and safety indices with and without maintenance interventions, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2, the performance profile of a deteriorating structure without maintenance is characterized by three parameters: initial performance index $P_0$, time to damage initiation $T_I$ until which no deterioration is present, and a constant deterioration rate α. Mathematically

$$P(t) = \begin{cases} P_0 & \text{if } t < t_i \\ P_0 - \alpha t & \text{if } t \geq t_i \end{cases} \tag{1}$$

The effects of a time-based maintenance action on the structure performance profile are described by the first application time $T_{PI}$, time interval of subsequent cyclic application $T_P$, immediate performance improvement γ, time delay of deterioration $T_D$, reduction of deterioration rate δ, and duration of maintenance effect $T_{PD}$ (Frangopol et al. 2001). The linear superposition rule is assumed applicable for computing the composite performance index profile of a structure with maintenance interventions (Kong and Frangopol 2003). Mathematically

where τ=relative time variable since application of maintenance. The max (·) operator excludes the numerical possibility of a negative net deterioration rate. Generally speaking, if there are *K* maintenance interventions active at the time of interest, the instantaneous net deterioration rate ε is calculated as

$$
\varepsilon=\max\left(\alpha-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\delta_i;0\right)
\tag{3}
$$

In contrast, performance-based maintenance interventions are applied when a performance index (condition or safety) reaches a predefined target value. Therefore, their times of application cannot be determined a priori. Other defining parameters as specified above still apply.

### Present Value of Cumulative Life-Cycle Maintenance Cost

Each maintenance intervention is associated with prescribed amount of monetary expenses that covers both direct and indirect costs. The effect of application time on the cost estimate can be accounted for by a discount rate that converts future cost into the value at a predefined reference time instant (usually the present time), i.e.

$$
C_{\text{PV}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{C_{t_i}}{(1+\nu)^{t_i}}
\tag{4}
$$

where $C_{\text{PV}}$=present value of the cumulative (life-cycle) cost due to all maintenance interventions; *N*=number of maintenance interventions; $C_{t_i}$=cost of maintenance interventions applied at time $t_i$; and ν=discount rate.

### Genetic Algorithms

Numerical algorithms are needed to automatically guide the search process for solution(s) that can optimize the predefined objective function(s). Different traditional algorithms are available for the existing bridge maintenance plannings that are formulated as optimization problems for life-cycle maintenance cost minimization only. These classical single-objective based algorithms, however, usually cannot directly handle multiple and conflicting objective functions such as the present problem where three distinct merit measures are treated separately as well as simultaneously in the optimization process.

The GAs are selected in this study as the primary optimization tool to solve the posed multiobjective optimal maintenance planning problem. The GAs are global stochastic search and optimization engines that mimic natural evolution and are based on Darwin's survival-of-the-fitness theory in the biological sciences (Goldberg 1989). They typically create improved solutions using three basic operators, i.e., selection, crossover, and mutation, until prescribed stopping criteria are considered met. Compared to most gradient-based traditional methods, GAs are problem-independent methods and do not rely on sensitivity information to update solutions. Most importantly, multiple competing objective functions can be easily handled by GAs using the concept of Pareto optimality. In addition, a set of optimal tradeoff solutions can be obtained with a single algorithm run by GA.

The GAs have found increasing popularity in a wide array of civil engineering applications due to their ease of implementation and robust performance. Most early applications focused on single objective-based optimization problems only. With the fruitful advancement of multiobjective GAs in the past decade (Deb 2001), studies on GA-based multiobjective optimization have appeared recently in a variety of civil engineering problems including urban planning (Balling et al. 1999), seismic design (Liu et al. 2004, 2005b), and bridge maintenance planning (Liu et al. 1997; Miyamoto et al. 2000; Furuta et al. 2004). More detailed description of multiobjective GAs can be found in Liu et al. (2005a,b).

When the condition index exceeds a target value of 3.0, the minor concrete repair strategy is applied to replace all cover concrete that has visual defects but not corroded reinforcing bars. Hence, the condition is significantly improved while the safety level remains unchanged; however, the deterioration in safety is delayed until the condition index drops to 1.0. The time- and performance-based cathodic protection is carried out when the condition index reaches 2.0, and it is then applied at a regular time interval; it delays deterioration of both condition and safety for 12.5 years upon application. The four maintenance strategies considered and their corresponding probabilistic condition, safety, and cost profiles are described in Neves and Frangopol (2005).

The design variables in this study are times of first and subsequent applications, $T_{pi}$ and $T_p$, of the silane treatment. They are encoded in GA as 10-bit binary strings, respectively. To start GA, an initial population of 1,000 solutions is randomly generated with a uniform distribution. Each of the subsequent generations comprises 100 offspring solutions and the nondominated elitist solutions from the previous generation. Fitness values of solutions are determined with a nondominated sorting technique that favors those closer to the global optimal front (i.e., the Pareto set) by assigning different ranks (Goldberg 1989) and then a niching

strategy known as the "crowding distance" measure that discriminates solutions of the same rank (Deb 2001). A constrained binary tournament selection scheme is used to reproduce fitter solutions as well as to handle constraint-violating solutions appropriately (Deb 2001). For the encoded binary representation of each design variable, one-point crossover is applied with a probability of 80% together with one-point mutation with a probability of 10% that helps to avoid premature convergence without adverse disruption in the presence of elitism.

Although uncertainties exist in predicting condition and safety profiles, a deterministic maintenance planning optimization with silane treatment only is carried out by using modal values of the random parameters in Tables 1 and 2. This provides a direct insight into how optimal planning of maintenance interventions can be obtained in the presence of multiple conflicting objective functions, without the interference of parameter uncertainties. Effects of these uncertainties on structure performance profiles with a maintenance solution obtained from the deterministic optimization is investigated afterwards, for which "rebuild," minor concrete repair, and cathodic protection strategies are considered as well.

### Optimization with Deterministic Parameters

The present GA-based procedure is now applied for optimal maintenance planning using silane. The time horizon is assumed

50 years and a constant discount rate of 6% (Tilly 1997) is used. As practical constraints on the performance objective functions, the condition index should not exceed 3.0 and the minimum acceptable safety index value is 0.91. A total of 1,851 optimized maintenance solutions are obtained at the 40th generation. These solutions exhibit a broad tradeoff distribution over the objective functions under consideration (Fig. 3). The maintenance cost objective in general conflicts with the condition objective: the less one spends on bridge maintenance, the larger (i.e., worse) the bridge condition becomes, and vice versa. Likewise, having a safer bridge naturally requires more maintenance cost. Although condition and safety objectives are usually interact, as indicated in Table 2, they are not necessarily fully correlated because they represent different performances of a bridge component; effects of silane treatment on these two indices are not the same either.

In contrast to traditional procedures that minimize life-cycle maintenance cost only while imposing performance constraints, the significance of the present multiobjective optimization formulation for bridge maintenance planning is that it provides bridge managers with a complete set of tradeoff alternative solutions without using predefined constraints on any important performance measures (except for those ensuring practically reasonable solutions). The traditional single optimal solution may be simply viewed as a single point in Fig. 3 by enforcing specific constraints on relevant objective functions. Facing this large pool of optimized alternative solutions, decision makers now have much flexibility to select the final compromise maintenance solution, depending on the relative importance they may place on various objectives under consideration.

A total of nine representative optimized solutions, labeled *A–I*, are identified in Fig. 3 with relevant information provided in Table 3 to illustrate how a final maintenance solution could be selected through a tradeoff analysis of the 1,851 optimized solutions. In the first situation, a bridge manager sets the threshold condition level to be 3.0 at a 50 year horizon while seeking for a preferred maintenance solution that balances maintenance expenses and safety level appropriately. Solutions *A*, *B*, and *C* are associated with practically the same condition objective value of 3.0. It is clear that these solutions exhibit different balances of cost and safety performances: an increase in safety requires additional investment in maintenances, given the presently fixed condition objective level of 3.0.

## Conclusions

In this paper, genetic algorithm based multiobjective optimization techniques have been applied for maintenance planning of deteriorating bridges. Structure condition, safety, and cumulative life-cycle maintenance cost have been considered as separate objective functions subject to simultaneous optimization. Unlike traditional bridge management system tools aiming to seek for a single maintenance solution that minimizes life-cycle maintenance cost alone, the present automated procedure produces a large pool of alternative maintenance solutions that establishes optimized tradeoff among all competing merit objectives. As a result, bridge managers have a much broader perspective of the entire scope of optimal maintenance solution options. Through an explicit tradeoff analysis, bridge managers are able to actively choose the final compromise maintenance solution that has desirable condition and safety levels with balanced life-cycle maintenance cost, other than passively accepting performance levels implied by the single solution resulting from the traditional long-term cost minimization only.

Monte Carlo simulation results have shown that uncertainties associated with the deterioration and maintenance processes may have significant effects on estimating performance profiles as well as life-cycle maintenance cost. Besides the time-based silane treatment used in the present optimization process, other performance-based maintenance strategies, including "rebuild," minor concrete repair, and cathodic protection may also be considered in order to obtain more practical and cost-effective maintenance solutions.

The writers have considered probabilistic structure performance profiles in bridge maintenance planning optimization (Liu and Frangopol 2004) and carried out annual maintenance prioritization using a variety of maintenance strategies (Liu and Frangopol 2005a). In addition to the above project-level bridge maintenance management for a group of similar structures, the writers have also investigated the network-level bridge maintenance management (Liu and Frangopol 2005b), in which limited resources are prioritized to bridges that form the critical nodes of a highway network in order to cost effectively maintain the satisfactory long-term performance of the entire network.
